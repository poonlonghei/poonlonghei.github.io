## An Invitation to Discover Monastic Life

There are moments in our spiritual journey when we are invited to pause, listen, and reflect more deeply upon the purpose of our lives. Sometimes, such invitations come through unexpected encounters, conversations, or opportunities to experience a different way of living. For me, the opportunity to spend several days with the Benedictine monks at Ealing Abbey in London, from 30 October to 2 November 2026, became one such occasion.

Having spent much of my life involved in the sacred liturgy and the ceremonial service of the Church, I have always been drawn to the beauty of Catholic worship, the solemnity of the Holy Mass, and the richness of the Church's liturgical tradition. Yet, beyond the external ceremonies and liturgical functions, there has also been a growing desire to understand more deeply the spiritual life that sustains the Church's prayer.

My visit to Ealing Abbey offered me an opportunity to encounter the Benedictine way of life, not merely through reading books or studying its history, but by experiencing something of the daily life of a monastic community.

Over the course of four days, I was able to participate in the rhythm of monastic life, sharing in the community's prayer, meals, and fraternal gatherings. More importantly, I had the opportunity to speak with several members of the community about their experiences and understanding of the monastic vocation.

## Nihil Operi Dei Præponatur: The Primacy of Prayer

One of the most beautiful principles of Benedictine spirituality is expressed in Chapter 43 of the Rule of St Benedict: *Nihil Operi Dei Præponatur* — *Let Nothing Be Preferred to the Work of God*.

The expression Opus Dei, literally meaning the Work of God, refers in this context to the Divine Office, the Church's daily liturgical prayer. For Benedictine monks, the celebration of the Divine Office is not simply one activity among many; it occupies a central place in the ordering of their communal life.

This principle invites us to reconsider the proper place of prayer in our own lives. In a world increasingly occupied by work, responsibilities, distractions, and the demands of everyday living, it is easy to allow other things to take precedence over our relationship with God.

The Benedictine tradition offers a different perspective. Prayer is not something to be fitted into whatever time remains after our daily responsibilities have been fulfilled. Rather, our lives and responsibilities should be ordered around our relationship with God.

During my stay at Ealing Abbey, I had the opportunity to pray the Divine Office alongside the monastic community. Participating in the daily cycle of the Church's liturgical prayer allowed me to appreciate more deeply the importance of the Divine Office in Benedictine life.

There is something particularly profound about gathering together in the monastery church, setting aside individual activities and responsibilities, and directing one's attention towards God through the psalms, readings, hymns, and prayers of the Church.

The Divine Office is not merely a private devotion or an individual spiritual exercise. It is the official prayer of the Church, offered in union with Christ, who continually intercedes for His people before the Father.

For the monks, this common prayer forms an essential part of their daily life. It gives structure and direction to the day, reminding them that their primary purpose is to seek God and offer Him worship.

Experiencing this rhythm of prayer firsthand reminded me that the worship of God is not simply one aspect of Christian life, but its very centre.

## Vita Communis: Experiencing the Life of Community

Monastic life is not simply a life of prayer lived in isolation. It is also a life of fraternity, in which men are called to seek God together within a religious community.

One of the most memorable aspects of my visit was the opportunity to experience the ordinary rhythm of community life at Ealing Abbey. Beyond praying the Divine Office, I was able to share meals with the monks and participate in their fraternal gatherings.

These moments may appear ordinary, but they reveal an important dimension of monastic spirituality. Sharing meals, exchanging conversations, and spending time together are not merely social activities; they are expressions of a common life founded upon charity, mutual respect, and the pursuit of God.

I particularly appreciated the opportunity to meet and speak with the monks in a more personal setting. Through these encounters, I began to understand that the life of a Benedictine monk involves much more than the celebration of the liturgy or the observance of a religious rule. It is a daily commitment to living with others, accepting their differences, serving their needs, and growing together in holiness.

The Benedictine understanding of community is deeply rooted in the virtues of humility, obedience, charity, and mutual service. Living together requires patience, understanding, and a willingness to place the needs of others before one's own preferences.

In Chapter 53 of the Rule of St Benedict, St Benedict teaches that all guests arriving at the monastery are to be received as Christ Himself. This understanding of hospitality reflects the words of Our Lord:

>*"I was a stranger and you welcomed me" (Matthew 25:35).*

I experienced something of this Benedictine hospitality during my stay. I was welcomed not simply as a visitor, but given the opportunity to share in the life of the community for several days.

It reminded me that Christian fraternity is not merely a matter of living under the same roof. It is a continual effort to recognise Christ in one another and to grow together in charity.

## Conversations with the Monastic Community

Another particularly meaningful part of my visit was the opportunity to speak with several of the monks about Benedictine monasticism and community life.

I was grateful to have conversations with Father Dominic, the Abbot of Ealing Abbey; Father Alexander, the Prior; and Father Martin, the former Abbot, who currently serves as Novice Master.

Each of these conversations offered me an opportunity to learn more about the different dimensions of monastic life and the responsibilities involved in sustaining a Benedictine community.

Speaking with Father Dominic gave me an opportunity to understand more about the responsibilities of an Abbot, whose role is not simply one of governance, but also of spiritual fatherhood and pastoral care for the monastic community. The Abbot is entrusted with guiding the monks in their common pursuit of God, following the spirit and teachings of the Rule of St Benedict.

My conversations with Father Alexander, the Prior, also helped me appreciate the importance of the organisation and daily administration of monastic life. The Prior assists the Abbot in the leadership of the community and helps ensure the proper ordering of its common life.

I was also particularly interested in speaking with Father Martin, the former Abbot and current Novice Master. His responsibility for the formation of novices provides an important insight into the process through which a man gradually learns to embrace the Benedictine vocation.

The formation of a monk is not simply an introduction to the customs, prayers, and observances of a monastery. It is a gradual process of spiritual and human formation, through which a novice learns to live according to the Rule of St Benedict and to seek God within the life of the community.

These conversations helped me appreciate that monastic life requires more than an initial enthusiasm or attraction to its spirituality. It demands perseverance, humility, obedience, patience, and a genuine willingness to surrender oneself to God.

I am particularly grateful to all three Fathers for their generosity in sharing their experiences and insights with me. Their conversations gave me much to reflect upon, especially concerning the relationship between personal spirituality, communal responsibility, and the discernment of a religious vocation.

## Ora et Labora: Prayer and Work

The familiar Benedictine expression Ora et Labora — Pray and Work — provides another important insight into the monastic vocation.

Although commonly associated with Benedictine spirituality, these words are not a direct quotation from the Rule of St Benedict. Nevertheless, they express an essential characteristic of the Benedictine way of life: the integration of prayer, work, and the ordinary responsibilities of daily living.

In the monastery, work is not understood as something separate from the spiritual life. Rather, when undertaken with the proper disposition, it becomes another means through which a person may serve God and contribute to the well-being of the community.

This principle is equally relevant to those living outside the monastery.

Not everyone is called to religious life, nor is everyone called to withdraw from the world. Nevertheless, every Christian is called to sanctify his or her ordinary circumstances through faithfulness, charity, and service.

Whether through professional responsibilities, apostolic activities, or the simple duties of everyday life, we are invited to offer our work to God.

For me, this raises an important question: how can I place God at the centre of my daily responsibilities, rather than allowing my responsibilities to become the centre of my life?

It is a question that deserves continual reflection.

## Discerning the Will of God

Perhaps the most personal aspect of this visit was the opportunity to reflect more deeply upon my own spiritual journey and vocation.

Discernment is not always an easy process. It requires patience, prayer, humility, and a willingness to surrender our own expectations to the will of God.

There are times when we may have particular plans or ambitions for our future, only to discover that God's providence may be leading us in another direction. Such moments invite us not to become discouraged, but to trust that the Lord knows what is best for us.

My experience at Ealing Abbey has encouraged me to consider more seriously the spiritual significance of monastic life and the possibility of a vocation within the Benedictine tradition.

However, I believe that authentic discernment must never be reduced to personal attraction or enthusiasm. A religious vocation is not simply a choice of lifestyle; it is a response to God's invitation, which requires prayer, spiritual guidance, and the careful consideration of the Church.

The Rule of St Benedict places great emphasis on discernment, moderation, obedience, and the gradual formation of the monk. It reminds us that the journey towards holiness is not accomplished overnight, but through daily fidelity to God.

I therefore return from this experience with gratitude, but also with a renewed desire to listen, pray, and seek the Lord's will.

## Returning Home: Continuing the Journey

As I returned to Walsingham following my stay at Ealing Abbey, I carried with me many reflections and memories of the hospitality and fraternity I had experienced.

Although the visit lasted only a few days, it offered me an opportunity to encounter something of the Benedictine spirit: a life ordered around prayer, sustained by fraternity, and expressed through humble service.

I am particularly grateful to Father Dominic, Father Alexander, Father Martin, and all the confrères at Ealing Abbey for their kindness, generosity, hospitality, and willingness to share something of their monastic life with me.

The opportunity to pray the Divine Office together, share meals, participate in fraternal gatherings, and speak with the Fathers about monastic and community life made these four days a memorable and meaningful experience.

More importantly, during my time at the Abbey, I devoted myself to prayer and personal reflection, seeking to listen more attentively to God's voice. Through these moments of prayer and reflection, I began to feel a growing conviction that the Lord might be calling me to embrace the Benedictine vocation and become a Benedictine monk.

This was a particularly profound moment in my spiritual journey. I came to realise that my attraction to monastic life might be more than an appreciation of its liturgical beauty, spirituality, and traditions. Perhaps it was an invitation to consider giving my entire life to God through prayer, obedience, fraternity, and the observance of the Rule of St Benedict.

I recognise, however, that discerning a religious vocation requires time, patience, spiritual guidance, and the careful consideration of the Church. A personal conviction must be tested and accompanied by proper discernment. I therefore wish to approach this possibility with humility, without rushing towards any conclusion, and with complete trust in Divine Providence.

I was especially grateful for Father Dominic's kind invitation to return to the Abbey for a further stay of approximately one week. Such an opportunity would allow me to experience more fully the daily rhythm of Benedictine monastic life, including the regular hours of prayer, communal meals, work, and fraternal fellowship. I would also welcome the opportunity to spend more time with the community, deepen my understanding of the Benedictine vocation, and continue reflecting upon my own spiritual discernment.

I received this invitation with great gratitude and hope to take up the opportunity when circumstances permit. I believe that a longer stay would provide a valuable occasion not only to learn more about monastic life, but also to spend more time in prayer and silence, listening more attentively to God's will.

Ultimately, the Benedictine principle *Nihil Operi Dei Præponatur* is not merely a rule governing the daily schedule of monks. It is also a profound spiritual reminder for every Christian: God must always occupy the first place in our lives.

Whether within the walls of a monastery or amid the responsibilities of ordinary life, we are all called to seek God above everything else.

As I continue this journey, I pray for the grace to recognise God's will, the courage to respond generously to His calling, and the humility to follow wherever He may lead me.

For whatever the future may hold, may everything in my life be ordered towards His greater glory.
