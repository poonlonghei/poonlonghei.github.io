## The hidden traditions of the Benedictine Divine Office

In the monastic tradition of the Catholic Church, the Divine Office (Divinum Officium), also known as the Liturgy of the Hours, is not merely a fixed schedule of daily prayer for religious, but the very heart of the common life of a monastic community. Particularly in the Benedictine tradition, prayer and work (Ora et Labora) are interwoven, making the life of a monk a continual act of worship offered to God.

Most recently, I had the privilege of experiencing monastic and communal life during my visit to Ealing Abbey, a Benedictine monastery in London, from 30 October to 2 November 2026. During my stay, I was able to experience firsthand the prayer and communal life of the Benedictine monks. This experience deepened my appreciation of the rhythm of prayer in the Benedictine Divine Office, particularly a Latin prayer recited at the conclusion of the Office:

> *Divinum auxilium maneat semper nobiscum. Et cum fratribus nostris absentibus. Amen.*

This brief prayer left a profound impression on me. It was through participating in the Divine Office at Ealing Abbey that I began to appreciate its deeper meaning: it is not merely a customary formula recited at the conclusion of daily prayer, but a beautiful expression of communal prayer, fraternal charity, and spiritual communion within the Benedictine monastic tradition.

## The Divine Office: The Heart of Monastic Life

In his Rule (Regula Benedicti), St Benedict places the Work of God (Opus Dei) at the very heart of monastic life.

Chapters 8 to 18 of the Rule lay down detailed provisions for the Night Office, Lauds, the Little Hours, and Vespers, ensuring that the daily life of the monks unfolds according to the rhythm of prayer.

In the Benedictine tradition, the Divine Office is not simply the fulfilment of a religious obligation, but the common participation of the entire monastic community in the prayer of Christ.

Through the Psalms, Scripture readings, hymns, and prayers, the monks offer their whole lives to God. Whether at the morning office of Lauds, the Hours during the day, or the evening office of Vespers, the monastic community, through the prayer of the Church, presents to God its daily work, joys, difficulties, and anxieties.

The Divine Office is therefore not merely an additional element of monastic life, but the very foundation of Benedictine communal life.

## Divinum auxilium: Dependence on God's Help

At the conclusion of the Divine Office, the monks recite together:

> *Divinum auxilium maneat semper nobiscum.*

> *May the divine assistance remain always with us.*

This prayer first reminds the religious that all prayer, work, and efforts in the monastic life must be founded upon the grace of God.

The Latin expression Divinum auxilium means "divine assistance", while maneat semper nobiscum means "may it remain always with us".

It is worth noting that this prayer does not declare, at the conclusion of the Divine Office, that the monks have completed their daily work of prayer. Rather, it entrusts the entire community anew to God.

Although the Divine Office comes to an end, dependence upon God does not.

After leaving the church, the monks must continue to seek the will of God through their work, service, silence, and communal life. This prayer therefore serves as a reminder that every aspect of monastic life must be sustained through divine assistance.

## Et cum fratribus nostris absentibus: In Communion with Our Absent Brethren

Perhaps the most distinctly Benedictine element of this prayer is the following phrase:

> *Et cum fratribus nostris absentibus.*

> *And with our absent brethern.*

During communal prayer, the monks ordinarily gather together in the church to celebrate the same Divine Office. However, monastic life does not always allow every brother to be physically present at every moment.

Some monks may be away undertaking pastoral duties; others may be confined to the infirmary because of ill health; still others may be unable to attend the communal Office owing to other legitimate responsibilities.

The Benedictine tradition does not, however, exclude them from the community's prayer.

On the contrary, when the monks mention their "absent brethren" at the conclusion of the Divine Office, they entrust those who are unable to be physically present in prayer to the assistance of God.

This prayer expresses an important aspect of the monastic spirit: a monastic community is not constituted solely by those who happen to be gathered in the church at that particular moment, but is a community whose members belong to one another in Christ.

Even when a brother is unable to be physically present, he remains a member of the monastery. Even when he is elsewhere, the community continues to pray for him.

This communion also recalls the life of the early Church described in the Acts of the Apostles:

> *"They devoted themselves to the teaching of the apostles and to the communal life, to the breaking of the bread and to the prayers." (Acts 2:42)*

Benedictine monastic life puts this ecclesial communion into practice through the community's daily common prayer.

## The Office Ends, but Communal Communion Does Not

In the Benedictine spirit, the conclusion of the Divine Office does not mean that the monks' relationship with their community is temporarily interrupted.

In his Rule, St Benedict emphasises that monks should practise mutual obedience, show reverence towards one another, and treat their brethren with the charity of Christ.

The prayer recited at the conclusion of the Divine Office encapsulates this very spirit in just a few words:

"May the divine assistance remain always with us, and with our brethren who are absent."

It reminds the religious that even when they undertake different tasks, and even when a brother is unable to participate in communal prayer for a particular reason, the entire community remains united in God.

This unity is not simply founded upon living together in the same monastery, but upon seeking God together, participating in the prayer of the Church, and bearing one another's burdens in charity.

The concluding prayer of the Divine Office is therefore not the end of communal life, but rather the beginning of carrying the grace received through common prayer into everyday life.

## Lessons for the Church and the Lives of the Faithful

The spirit expressed in the Benedictine tradition of the Divine Office is not confined to monasteries, but can also inspire the life of the whole Church.

Within the Church, it is impossible for us to be physically gathered with all our brothers and sisters at every moment. Nevertheless, through prayer, we can continue to support one another and pray for those who are ill, lonely, far from home, or unable to participate in communal life for various reasons.

This prayer is particularly meaningful for the faithful who find themselves living in different places, far away from their original parishes or communities. It reminds us that ecclesial communion is not restricted by geographical boundaries.

We may be in different places, yet we remain united with one another in Christ.

At the conclusion of the Divine Office, Benedictine monks entrust their absent brethren to the assistance of God. In the same way, all Christians should bring their brothers and sisters before God, allowing prayer to become a tangible expression of mutual love.

## In Christ, We Are Never Truly Alone

This prayer is one of the precious spiritual insights I gained during my stay at Ealing Abbey. It reminded me that true communal communion does not depend upon whether every brother is physically present, but is founded upon seeking God together, praying for one another, and bearing one another in charity within Christ.

When the Divine Office concludes and the monks leave the church to return to their respective duties and daily lives, this communion continues.

Although brief, the prayer at the conclusion of the Benedictine Divine Office contains a profound monastic wisdom. It reminds us that God is at the centre of monastic life, charity is the foundation of communal life, and true communion is not interrupted by the absence of any brother.

As the monks recite this prayer together, they not only ask that the divine assistance may remain always with them, but also entrust those brethren who are unable to be physically present in prayer to the loving care of God.

This prayer thus stands as a beautiful witness to Benedictine communal life: in God, we are never truly alone.
